Does advertising expand the market for hospital services? Evidence from Medicare.

Journal of health economics · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

Direct-to-consumer advertising is pervasive in US healthcare markets, but little evidence exists on the effects of advertising by hospitals, second only to drug manufacturers in medical marketing. Advertising may help facilities increase market share by stealing existing patients, expanding the market for hospital care, or both. Regardless, it has important public finance implications due to the large sums of taxpayer funds spent by federal and state governments to subsidize hospital operations and finance care through public insurance programs. This paper provides the first causal evidence on the market expansion effects of hospital advertising. To obtain causal estimates, we leverage the fact that spikes in political advertising significantly crowd out hospital advertising in the same market, motivating an instrumental variables design. Using claims data on the universe of Traditional Medicare beneficiaries, we find that advertising expands aggregate patient volume and spending on inpatient care - though to a modest degree (implied elasticities are 0.06 and 0.05, respectively). Although the overall effect of advertising on hospital outpatient care is small and imprecise, for-profit hospitals obtain higher outpatient Medicare volume and revenue.

Neural Response to Monetary and Social Feedback in Childhood: Processing Reward and Loss in Relation to Peer Outcomes and Anxiety Symptoms.

Biological psychology · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

Anxiety disorders are the most prevalent psychiatric conditions in childhood and are associated with social functioning impairment. How youth process rewards may contribute to the development of social impairment in childhood anxiety. Although aberrant reward processing is a well-established mechanism of psychopathology, associations with anxiety remain inconsistent and limited, especially in childhood. This study investigated neural response to monetary and social reward feedback in relation to anxiety and peer functioning in children. Children (N=106; Age=8.12&#xb1;.68 years) completed the Doors Task and the Island Getaway Task while electroencephalogram (EEG) recordings assessed event-related potentials (ERPs) to wins and losses. Parents completed questionnaires assessing child anxiety, depression, and peer relations. Contrary to pre-registered hypotheses, we found a significant Valence (reward versus loss) by Condition (monetary versus social) interaction. Specifically, children exhibited greater neural response to monetary loss than reward, but showed the opposite pattern in the social condition, with greater neural response to social reward than loss. Higher child- and parent-reported total and generalized anxiety predicted significantly greater neural response to monetary reward, but not monetary loss or social feedback. As hypothesized, anxiety symptoms significantly moderated the association between neural response and peer relations, demonstrating that for highly anxious children, a greater neural response to social loss predicted worse peer relations. Findings demonstrate differences in neural response to monetary and social feedback processing. Moreover, the distinct vulnerability to social rejection might impair peer functioning in anxious children, suggesting that clinical interventions targeting social rejection processing may effectively improve peer outcomes in childhood anxiety.

Subjective emotional ratings dissociate brain activation and network patterns during counterfactual outcome processing.

NeuroImage · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

Human decision-making integrates outcome evaluation, counterfactual comparison, and individual traits. We investigated the neural processing of outcome-related emotions during economic decision-making by classifying feedback according to participants' subjective emotional ratings rather than objective gain-loss categories. Participants completed a Wheel of Fortune gambling task during fMRI scanning and provided emotional ratings for feedback outcomes, enabling identification of regret, relief, disappointment, and elation. We also assessed individual differences in regret proneness, maximization tendencies, alternative search, high standards, and decision difficulty. Behaviorally, emotional ratings increased with larger obtained outcomes and decreased as reference outcomes increased, indicating robust modulation of outcome evaluation by counterfactual information Whole-brain analyses revealed greater activation for low-rating than high-rating outcomes, indicating stronger neural responses to negative than positive emotions across feedback types. Complete feedback also elicited greater activation than partial feedback within the same emotional valence. Network-level analyses revealed partially dissociable neural profiles for positive and negative emotions. Positive emotions, including elation and relief, predominantly recruited the somatomotor network, whereas negative emotions, including disappointment and regret, engaged broader distributions across the default mode, dorsal attention, and other large-scale networks. Regret elicited the largest activation extent, whereas elation elicited the smallest. Region-of-interest analyses showed that bilateral orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) responses were significantly greater for negative than positive emotions and for complete than partial feedback conditions. Across all four emotions, right OFC activation was negatively associated with maximization. For elation, relief, and disappointment, right OFC activation was also negatively associated with alternative search and decision difficulty, whereas these associations were absent for regret. Together, these findings demonstrate that subjective emotional evaluation, counterfactual context, and decision-related traits jointly shape the neural processing of decision outcomes, while suggesting relatively trait-independent neural processing of regret.

Circadian and Sleep-Wake Modulation of EEG Functional Connectivity Across Brain States and Its Associations With Cognition in Humans.

NeuroImage · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

Sleep and circadian rhythms both influence human brain function, but their distinct contributions to large-scale network organization remain unclear. We quantified the effect of brain state (wake, NREM, REM), sleep-wake history, and circadian phase on functional connectivity using over 1,200 hours of artifact-free EEG recorded during a 10-day forced-desynchrony protocol. We derived principal components of functional connectivity and found that both sleep-wake history and circadian phase significantly modulated functional connectivity, with sleep-wake history exerting a broader influence. Complementary cosinor analyses further demonstrated circadian rhythmicity in several NREM and REM connectivity measures. Across sleep, global connectivity increased progressively during NREM sleep, particularly in the delta and sigma bands, but declined during REM sleep. During controlled resting wakefulness, increasing time awake was associated with higher global alpha-band connectivity, which was associated with poorer concurrent cognitive performance. Connectivity during NREM sleep was also associated with subsequent cognitive performance, with lower global theta and, to a lesser extent, higher sigma connectivity linked to better performance. REM connectivity was not associated with these cognitive measures. Together, these findings reveal opposing NREM and REM trajectories in large-scale EEG functional connectivity and show that these brain-state-specific network dynamics are shaped by both sleep-wake history and circadian phase and are associated with cognitive performance.

Amplitude-Conditioned Brain Dynamics Reveal Widespread Negative Modulation and Targeted Positive Modulation of Functional Networks.

NeuroImage · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

Functional connectivity is traditionally characterized through pairwise interactions between brain regions. However, growing evidence suggests that neural communication is also shaped by higher-order interactions in which the activity of a third region modulates the coupling between others. Here, we introduce a whole-brain framework for mapping resting-state nonlinear conditional connectivity from functional MRI. For each cortical region, we quantified how spontaneous amplitude fluctuations modulate the functional connectivity of all other region pairs. This procedure generated a modulation spectrum for every connection, describing connectivity changes across multiple amplitude states of the modulator. From these spectra, we derived four interpretable features capturing modulatory strength, polarity, volatility, and nonlinear complexity. The framework was applied to resting-state data from the Human Connectome Project and evaluated using stationary surrogate models, spatial topography analyses, test-retest reliability assessments, and behavioral prediction. Surrogate analyses demonstrated that the observed modulation spectra could not be explained by static covariance structure or spectral properties alone, indicating genuine state-dependent dynamic interactions. Topographic mapping reveals a profound functional asymmetry: the macroscopic connectome is dominated by widespread negative modulatory influences, with positive modulation restricted to sparse assortative pathways. Furthermore, by evaluating the effect of temporal integration, we show that spatial and temporal aggregation robustly stabilizes these dynamic fluctuations into reliable, individual-specific traits. Finally, employing regularized predictive modeling, we demonstrate that these dynamic modulatory features can provide complementary predictive value beyond conventional static FC, particularly for capturing state-dependent affective traits such as internalizing behavior. Together, this framework unveils a structured, asymmetric, and behaviorally relevant higher-order modulatory architecture.

Multivariate Prediction of White Matter BOLD Activity and Its Relationship to Marginal Connectivity.

NeuroImage · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

Functional MRI studies have traditionally focused on gray matter, whereas white matter BOLD signals have often been treated as weak or artifactual. However, white matter BOLD fluctuations are robustly detectable and contain reproducible functional information, yet most gray-to-white matter analyses rely on only marginal functional connectivity, which cannot separate pairwise coupling from shared variance among distributed cortical systems. Here, we used a multivariate cortical prediction framework to test whether spontaneous white matter BOLD activity can be predicted from distributed cortical gray matter BOLD activity, and how this predictive structure relates to marginal connectivity. Resting-state fMRI data from 81 Human Connectome Project young adult participants were analyzed using a strict white matter mask with no overlap with cortical predictors. For each white matter voxel, time series from 400 cortical parcels were used to predict white matter BOLD signals with nested leave-one-run-out ridge regression. Cortical BOLD activity modestly but reliably predicted white matter BOLD dynamics, demonstrating consistent cross-validated prediction accuracy across a broad spatial extent of the white matter. Ridge &#x3b2; coefficients strongly recapitulated marginal functional connectivity fingerprints, indicating a shared functional backbone, but their first gradients differed reproducibly. The difference between these gradients defined a &#x3b2;-minus-FC G1 divergence axis that remained stable after broadly controlling for anatomical, signal-related, and spatial factors. At the low end of the axis, enriched in callosal and capsular structures, pairwise gray-to-white matter connectivity was relatively strong, but white matter BOLD fluctuations were predicted less well by distributed cortical BOLD activity than that connectivity would suggest. At the high end, enriched in posterior projection and association pathways, pairwise connectivity was relatively weak, but distributed cortical BOLD activity predicted white matter BOLD signals better than expected. These findings identify a reproducible, tract-organized contrast between marginal connectivity and its covariance-adjusted predictive representation.

Parietal Structural and Functional Differentiation Associated with Visual Tracking Across Motor Participation Profiles.

NeuroImage · 2026-10-19

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

This study aimed to examine whether structural and resting-state functional measures of parietal subregions differ across predefined motor participation groups with comparable physical activity levels, and whether these neural measures are associated with visual tracking performance. We adopted an a priori motor-cognitive grouping framework and recruited athletes accordingly, using two representative sports as operational exemplars for each profile: (1) externally driven spatial updating and action planning (ESU; basketball and taekwondo), (2) body-environment calibration and rhythmic sensorimotor integration (SCP; skating and running), and 3) internally guided control and sustained attentional precision (IPS; tai chi and shooting). A sedentary control group (CON) with low habitual physical activity was also recruited. In total, 80 college students participated. Physical activity (PA) was assessed using a standardized questionnaire. All participants underwent structural MRI and resting-state fMRI to derive gray matter volume (GMV), fractional amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (fALFF), degree centrality (DC), regional homogeneity (ReHo), and functional connectivity (FC) within parietal subregions (S1, SPL, AG, and SMG). Supplementary SPL-based FC analyses further examined connectivity with regions of the broader visuospatial and sensorimotor network. Visual tracking was assessed using a multiple object tracking (MOT) task across three difficulty levels. PA levels were significantly higher in all athlete groups than in sedentary controls, with no differences among athlete groups. Across structural and resting-state functional measures, the most consistent group differences were selectively concentrated in S1 and SPL, whereas effects involving other parietal regions were less consistent across measures. Specifically, the ESU group exhibited the highest bilateral S1 GMV, the SCP group showed the highest bilateral SPL GMV, and the IPS group demonstrated the highest fALFF in both S1 and SPL. The IPS group also showed the highest ReHo in bilateral SPL. FC differed significantly across several parietal connections, with distinct connectivity patterns across the three athlete groups. MOT accuracy differed significantly across groups at all difficulty levels, with the ESU and IPS groups outperforming the SCP and CON groups overall. Across all participants, multiple S1 and SPL metrics showed modest associations with MOT performance (r&#x202f;=&#x202f;0.20-0.40). Within athlete subgroups, stronger and task-specific associations were observed, particularly in the SCP and IPS groups (r&#x202f;=&#x202f;0.50-0.80). These findings identify distinct structural and functional patterns predominantly involving S1 and SPL across different motor participation profiles, despite comparable overall physical activity levels among the athlete groups. Such parietal differences are accompanied by difficulty-dependent differences in visual tracking performance and heterogeneous brain-behavior associations. Together, the results highlight S1 and SPL as the parietal regions showing the most consistent differentiation across structural and functional measures in association with distinct motor participation profiles and visual tracking performance, and provide a focused empirical basis for future investigations into the sources of this heterogeneity.
