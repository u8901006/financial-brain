A Blessing in Disguise: The Long-Term Effects of Childhood Exposure to the Boll Weevil on Old-Age Male Longevity.

Health economics · 2026-11-16

分類：Fallback

中文摘要

The infamous agricultural pest, the boll weevil, spread across southern US counties between 1892 and 1920. It had devastating impacts on cotton production, a highly child-labor-intensive crop. This study examines how the arrival of the boll weevil and the subsequent reductions in child labor affect later-life mortality outcomes. Using Social Security Administration death records linked with the 1940 census, we document that the staggered spread of the boll weevil improved the longevity of exposed male children. Full exposure to the boll weevil is associated with an intent-to-treat effect of 1-3&#xa0;months of higher longevity. We show that the estimated effects are robust across a wide array of specifications, are not driven by sociodemographic and socioeconomic compositional change in the final sample, and are not associated with pre-existing trends in the outcome. Further, we find larger impacts among Blacks, children of lower educated mothers, whose fathers work in the farming industry, and those born in high cotton intensity counties. Moreover, the effects appear to be substantially larger among children in states with less restrictive child labor policies, implying the role of these policies in reducing the adverse effects of child labor in the absence of the boll weevil in cotton production counties. Affected children are more likely to migrate, and their destination counties are more likely to be urban and have higher socioeconomic scores. The 1940 census suggests that the exposed children attain higher levels of education and have higher socioeconomic scores. We discuss the policy implications of these results in relation to child labor and school attendance policies in developing countries.
